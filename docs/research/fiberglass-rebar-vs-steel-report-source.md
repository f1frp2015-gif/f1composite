# Fiberglass Rebar vs Steel — Research Source

Audience: civil and structural engineers, asset owners, contractors, and procurement teams evaluating internal reinforcement for concrete.

Research date: 2026-08-31

## Scope and assumptions

- The comparison is between solid, round GFRP reinforcing bars and conventional carbon-steel reinforcing bars used internally in concrete.
- North American standards and public transportation research are the primary evidence base. Project engineers must still apply the governing local code and project specification.
- General material behavior is kept separate from product- and batch-specific test results.
- Cost figures are study inputs from one Minnesota bridge-deck analysis, not current market quotations or universal lifecycle guarantees.
- BFRP, CFRP, prestressing systems, smooth dowels, hybrid bars, and externally bonded strengthening systems are outside the core comparison.

## Direct answer

GFRP is usually the stronger candidate when chloride corrosion, electromagnetic transparency, electrical isolation, or reinforcement weight drives whole-life value. Steel remains the more direct candidate when stiffness, ductile yielding, field fabrication, elevated-temperature behavior, or conventional detailing controls. The materials are not interchangeable by equal bar area: GFRP is substantially lighter and does not participate in the steel-rust mechanism, but its tensile modulus is much lower and it remains linear-elastic until rupture rather than developing a steel-like yield plateau.

## Evidence synthesis

### Mechanical behavior

- A University of Kentucky Transportation Center/FHWA study tested four 15 mm-diameter GFRP bars and three 16 mm-diameter epoxy-coated steel bars. Mean ultimate tensile strength was 612 MPa for the tested GFRP and 653.4 MPa for the tested steel; the steel mean yield strength was 486.9 MPa. Mean tensile modulus was about 40.0 GPa for GFRP and 196 GPa for steel. These are results for the tested products, not universal minimums.
- The same report listed specific gravity of 1.92 for its GFRP bar and 7.85 for steel, implying a 75.5% lower density for the studied GFRP. It also listed 0.37 kg/m for a 15 mm GFRP bar and 1.552 kg/m for a 16 mm steel bar, but that unit-mass comparison uses different diameters and should not be presented as like-for-like.
- An Iowa State Institute for Transportation/MnDOT program tested four samples from the installed GFRP bar lot. Mean ultimate stress was 140.6 ksi (about 969 MPa), mean ultimate strain 17,994 microstrain (about 1.80%), and mean tensile modulus 7,869.7 ksi (about 54.3 GPa). The report explicitly observed linear stress increase to sudden failure without yielding.
- The University of Miami Structures and Materials Laboratory, operating under an ISO/IEC 17025:2017 quality system and identified in the source as IAS-accredited and FDOT-qualified, tested three 2019 production lots of No. 8 GFRP bar for a seawall. Mean guaranteed tensile load was 103.1 kip (458.6 kN) and mean modulus was 7,950 ksi (54.8 GPa). The result is size-, product- and project-specific; ASTM qualification reports tensile force by bar size rather than one universal strength.
- A separate FHWA thermal experiment measured average longitudinal coefficients of thermal expansion of about 9.18 microstrain/°C for the tested GFRP No. 4 bar and 11.88 microstrain/°C for the tested Grade 60 steel No. 4 bar over three measurement runs.

### Corrosion and field durability

- FHWA explains that chloride reaching conventional reinforcement can disrupt the passive film on steel, after which corrosion products lead to concrete cracking and spalling.
- A USDOT-funded collaboration among Missouri S&T, University of Miami, Penn State, and RE-CAST extracted 102 mm concrete cores from eleven U.S. bridges after 15–20 years of service. The structures experienced wet/dry cycles, freezing/thawing, and deicing salts. The reported tensile-stress reduction was 2.13% over 17 years; SEM/EDS found minimal physical damage of 0.05–0.12% in some bridges. Horizontal shear results were inconclusive, and some pristine baseline data were unavailable, so the result is encouraging field evidence rather than a universal service-life guarantee.
- Iowa State/MnDOT monitored side-by-side bridge decks—one GFRP-reinforced and one epoxy-coated-steel-reinforced—for close to four years. Both behaved consistently with design; both developed broadly similar surface and full-depth crack patterns. The GFRP deck had slightly higher strain, but the report judged the difference too small to be notable over the bridge lifetime. Four years is explicitly described by the authors as only a snapshot relative to an anticipated 75-plus-year life.

### Cost

- The MnDOT study used approximate agency costs and engineering assumptions in a 100-year present-value analysis of the two bridge decks, not the full bridges.
- Study inputs were $36/ft² initial construction for the epoxy-coated-steel deck and $42/ft² for the GFRP deck; reinforcement itself was $9/ft² versus $15/ft².
- For a 65-year service-life scenario, the reported lifecycle present value was $87.14/ft² for the steel deck and $67.22/ft² for GFRP when no extra GFRP repair was assumed. With a common 30-year repair added to GFRP, its 65-year result became $85.21/ft².
- The analysis is highly assumption-sensitive. It should be shown as a study scenario, never as a current price promise or a blanket percentage saving.

### Codes and qualification

- ACI CODE-440.11-22 is the consensus building-code route for covered concrete systems reinforced with qualifying solid, round, vinyl-ester GFRP bars. Its design model addresses strength, serviceability, durability, development, splicing, inspection, and elevated-temperature/fire requirements.
- ASTM D7957/D7957M-25 is the current GFRP bar product specification verified during this research. It covers qualification, production-lot control, physical properties, tensile force/modulus/strain, transverse shear, bond, and minimum bend diameter for cut lengths and bent shapes.
- ASTM D7205/D7205M-26 is the current tensile test method verified during this research. ASTM explicitly states that the method provides short-term static properties and does not account for sustained or fatigue loading.
- ASTM A615/A615M-26 is the current carbon-steel reinforcing-bar product specification verified during this research.
- ACI SPEC-440.5-22 covers construction of internally GFRP-reinforced concrete members, including submittals, storage, handling, placement, tolerances, and inspection.
- UFC 3-301-01, Appendix G (Change 5, 2026) provides a current U.S. Department of Defense design and construction boundary. It prohibits field bending, ignores GFRP compression strength in covered design, limits sustained stress to 0.3 of ultimate tensile stress, and restricts GFRP use in fire-rated or seismic-force-resisting systems under specified DoD conditions. Those restrictions are owner policy for DoD work, not a universal worldwide ban.

## Material limitations and disagreements

- “GFRP is stronger than steel” is too broad. Some GFRP grades have higher ultimate tensile strength than common steel yield strength, but ultimate-to-ultimate results vary by bar grade and test method; the Kentucky test lot had a slightly lower mean ultimate strength than its steel comparator.
- GFRP eliminates steel rust, not all concrete deterioration. Shrinkage, thermal restraint, poor consolidation, alkali/moisture/temperature effects, bond problems, impact damage, fire, and incorrect detailing remain relevant.
- Positive 15–20-year field findings do not prove a 75- or 100-year service life for every bar system. Resin chemistry, glass type, cure, surface profile, sustained stress, temperature, and qualification matter.
- The 2020 extracted-bar report's low 17-year tensile-strength reduction estimate came from a limited, nonstandard sliced-coupon correlation. A 2022 ASCE discussion challenged the optimistic interpretation and argued that an outer-versus-center comparison suggested a larger reduction. The webpage therefore treats the multi-bridge observations as encouraging but does not repeat a 100-year strength projection.
- Lifecycle cost is project-specific. The Minnesota result changes materially when a repair is added to the GFRP scenario.
- The webpage must identify institutional test results as research benchmarks and direct buyers to project-specific certified values for the offered production grade.

## Discovery record and stopping reason

Discovery covered official ACI and ASTM standard pages, FHWA research pages, the USDOT ROSA P repository, state DOT guidance, and university/DOT laboratory and field reports. Follow-up targeted exact sample counts, test values, lifecycle assumptions, current standard editions, and adverse evidence. Research stopped when every consequential page section had primary evidence and the remaining gaps—universal service life, current market cost, and offered-product guaranteed values—were explicitly bounded rather than estimated.
